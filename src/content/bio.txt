During undergrad, whenever I showed my mother a fancy robot demo or one of my own research videos, her very Bengali response was often: “কাজের কাজ কী? আমার রান্নাঘরের কাজ করে দেবে?” (“But what useful thing does it actually do? Will it do my kitchen chores?”). I think that question stuck with me more than I realized. I spend an unreasonable amount of time wondering why things that feel effortless to us, like turning a cap, passing an object, or simply using two hands together, remain remarkably difficult for robots.

That curiosity has pulled me toward manipulation, and especially toward understanding how robots coordinate their bodies, make and break contact, reason about what they can physically do, and keep functioning when the world refuses to behave exactly as expected. I like thinking about robots in the wonderfully messy part of the real world, where contact changes, objects slip, models are imperfect, and things rarely go exactly according to plan.

I’m especially drawn to ideas that sit somewhere between structure and learning: using what we already know about geometry, motion, forces, and dynamics, without pretending we can model everything. Neural networks and more data are incredibly powerful tools, but I’m not quite convinced that every robotics problem is secretly waiting for a bigger model and a larger dataset. Ultimately, I’m interested in building robots that are a little less clumsy, a lot more capable, and perhaps one day good enough to survive my mother’s kitchen test.
